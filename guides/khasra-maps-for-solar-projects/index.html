Khasra maps for solar and wind projects: what developers need

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 20-10-2026

Every utility-scale solar or wind project starts with land. Before a single module is designed, the land team must know which khasras the project covers, who owns them, which are government land and how the plots sit on the ground. A georeferenced khasra map answers all of that on one screen.

1. Identify the land inside the project boundary

Overlay the project KML on the georeferenced khasra layer and you get the exact list of khasras inside it - with their areas in an Excel sheet. That list drives title search, negotiations and the land budget.

2. Separate private and government land

Government land is often the fastest route to a large contiguous site. A colour-coded land category map shows private / khatedari land, non-allotable government land and allotable government land khasra by khasra, so allotment applications can target the right plots.

3. Aggregate contiguous parcels

Because neighbouring villages are joined edge to edge in one combined layer, you can see contiguous blocks across village boundaries and plan acquisition in phases.

4. Design the layout on real geometry

The DWG / DXF files are in real UTM coordinates, so the engineering team designs module tables, roads and the substation directly on the khasra boundaries - and knows which plots each structure touches.

5. Plan the transmission route

For the evacuation line, the khasra layer along the corridor shows every plot the route crosses, which helps compensation planning and right-of-way.

6. Verify on the ground

Digitized land-record maps are excellent for planning but they are not a legal survey. For boundaries that matter legally, a DGPS or Total Station demarcation survey marks the khasra corners on the ground - with the georeferenced map as the base.

What to ask your map provider

  • Every khasra as a closed polygon with its number - no overlaps, no gaps
  • Real-world coordinates (WGS 84 / UTM), aligned to satellite imagery
  • KML for the land team, DWG for engineering, SHP for GIS, Excel for the land budget
  • Village boundaries and one combined project layer
  • Land category (private / government / allotable) where records allow
