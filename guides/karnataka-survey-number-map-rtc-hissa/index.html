Karnataka survey number map: how to read the village map with the RTC and hissa numbers

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 05-11-2026

In Karnataka, land is identified by a survey number, and a part of a survey number by a hissa number. Where other states speak of a khasra, a Karnataka record speaks of survey number 112, hissa 3, or simply 112/3. The village map shows where that land lies; the RTC says what it is and who holds it. This guide explains how to read the map, how to connect it with the records, and what to do when you need the land on a satellite image or in a GIS file.

1. Where the map comes from

Karnataka village maps come from the old revenue surveys and are kept by the Department of Survey, Settlement and Land Records. Revenue work runs through the district, the taluk, the hobli (a group of villages under a revenue inspector) and the village, where the village accountant keeps the local records. The record kept by the revenue office is what counts in a transaction.

2. The numbers that identify a plot

  • Village, hobli and taluk: the same survey number exists in every village, so always note them with it.
  • Survey number: the main unit on the village map.
  • Hissa number: a sub-division of a survey number, written as 112/3 or 112/3A. A hissa can be divided again.
  • Surnoc: a short number added in the records to some survey numbers to show a later change; it must match exactly when you read the RTC.

3. Reading the village map

The village map shows each survey number as a closed shape with its number, together with roads, cart tracks, streams, tanks and the gaothan (the settled part of the village). The hissa divisions inside a survey number are often not on the village map; they are drawn on a separate sketch of that survey number, sometimes called the hissa or tippan sketch. So a village map tells you which survey number a point falls in, and the survey sketch tells you which hissa.

4. Linking the map to the RTC

RTC stands for Record of Rights, Tenancy and Crops, and is also called the pahani. It is plot-wise: for each survey and hissa number it gives the extent, the holders, the kind of land and other entries. Take the survey number, hissa and surnoc from the map or sketch, choose the same district, taluk, hobli and village, and open the RTC. Changes after a sale, inheritance or partition are recorded through mutation, so always read the current RTC, and check older ones when you trace a title back.

5. Kharab and government land entries

Karnataka records use the word kharab for land within a survey number that is not counted as cultivable. In general, A kharab is land unfit for cultivation, such as rock or a ditch, while B kharab is land set aside for public use, such as a path, a tank or a burial ground. The records also show government land and land reserved for village needs such as grazing (gomala). These entries affect what can be done with the land, so read the current record and take advice from a revenue professional before relying on them. Our guide on checking government land in a village covers the wider method.

6. Units of area

Karnataka records give area in acres and guntas, where 40 guntas make 1 acre, so one gunta is about 101 square metres and one acre is about 0.405 hectare. Some records also show annas for smaller parts. Always convert from the recorded value, not from a spoken figure, and state the conversion you used in any land schedule.

7. Why a viewer map is not yet a GIS map

A map on a screen is good for finding a survey number, but it is not a file you can open with GPS points or a project layout. For that, the map must be georeferenced, meaning fitted to real coordinates such as WGS 84 latitude / longitude or UTM metres, and each survey number digitized as a closed polygon carrying its number. The result should be checked on satellite imagery across the whole village: field edges, tracks, tank bunds and streams on the image should run along the survey lines.

8. Common uses

  • Seeing a survey number on a satellite image in Google Earth
  • Checking which survey numbers fall inside a proposed solar, wind or industrial site
  • Listing the survey numbers crossed by a road, pipeline or transmission line route
  • Preparing a survey-wise land schedule with village, extent and land type

9. Limits to keep in mind

The map shows the record as drawn. Recent hissa divisions and new roads may not appear on the village map, and neighbouring village maps do not always agree exactly along their shared edge. A digitized map is a planning base, not proof of title. The revenue record remains the legal source, and where a line decides ownership or compensation, a DGPS or Total Station survey on the ground is the next step.
