District-wise cadastral maps for solar projects: searching land across a whole tehsil

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 29-10-2026

A solar park of a few hundred megawatts needs one compact block of land, and that block rarely sits inside one village. Before anyone visits the field, the land team has to look at many villages together: which plots are private, which are government land, where the roads and substations are, and where the plots join into one usable shape. That is a job for district-wise or tehsil-wise cadastral maps rather than single village sheets. This guide explains what such a map set is, how it is put together and what to check before you rely on it.

1. Why one village at a time is not enough

  • A good site often crosses a village boundary - the best block may lie half in one village and half in the next.
  • Government land, grazing land and private holdings are scattered; only a wide view shows where they form a large block.
  • Evacuation matters as much as land: the distance to a substation or a transmission line decides the cost of the project.
  • Comparing tehsils early saves field visits to areas that never had enough land.

2. What a district-wise map set contains

Each village map is digitized plot by plot: every khasra (or survey, gat or dag number) becomes a closed polygon carrying its number, and the village outline is built from the outer plots. All villages are then georeferenced in one coordinate system, WGS 84 latitude / longitude for Google Earth or UTM metres for engineering, so that neighbouring villages meet along a common edge instead of overlapping or leaving gaps. The result can be opened as one layer for a whole tehsil or district.

3. How the villages are joined

Neighbouring village maps were drawn at different times, sometimes by different survey parties, so their shared edges do not always agree. When villages are joined, the edges are matched so that a plot belongs to exactly one village and no area is counted twice. Each village keeps its own folder and file names, and a combined file shows the whole area at once. Checking every village against satellite imagery - roads, canals and field bunds should run along the plot lines - catches a village that has been placed wrongly.

4. Adding land categories

A plain plot map shows shapes, not status. For site search the useful step is to colour the plots by land category from the record of rights: private (khatedari) land, government land that may be allotted, land that cannot be allotted such as roads, ponds, grazing land or forest, and land already leased or allotted. Seen across a district, these colours show at a glance where a large block of one category exists. The categories come from the public record and should be read as a first screen, not as a final legal position.

5. Screening checks before shortlisting a block

  1. Shape - is the block compact enough for module rows, or broken by narrow plots and roads?
  2. Exclusions - water bodies, drains, forest, habitation and religious sites inside or next to the block.
  3. Access - an approach road, and the route for the internal and evacuation lines.
  4. Terrain - slope and low-lying ground from an elevation model; flooded land is costly to build on.
  5. Neighbours - plots that cut into the block and would need separate agreements.

6. The files a land team needs

  • KML / KMZ - for Google Earth, to share the area with management and field staff.
  • DWG / DXF - for the layout engineers in AutoCAD or BricsCAD, in UTM metres.
  • SHP - for QGIS or ArcGIS, with khasra number, village and category in the attribute table.
  • Excel - a khasra-wise list with village, area and category, which becomes the land schedule later.

7. Limits to keep in mind

Cadastral maps show the record as it was drawn; recent sub-divisions, conversions and new roads may not appear yet, and records are updated at different speeds in different districts. A georeferenced map set is a planning base. Once a block is shortlisted, the record of rights for each plot is checked with the revenue office, and a DGPS or Total Station survey fixes the corners on the ground before land is bought or leased.
