Maharashtra gat number map: how to read it with the 7/12 extract

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 01-11-2026

In rural Maharashtra, land is identified by a gat number or a survey number. The number appears in two places: on the village map, which shows where the plot lies, and on the 7/12 extract (satbara), which records what the plot is and who holds it. Neither answers every question alone. This guide explains how to read the map, how to connect it with the 7/12, and what to do when you need the plot on a satellite image or in a GIS file.

1. Where the map comes from

Village maps come from land surveys and later consolidation work, and they are maintained by the revenue and land records departments. At village level, the talathi keeps the village records; the taluka and district offices sit above that. The record kept by the revenue office is what counts in a transaction.

2. Gat number or survey number?

Both words identify a plot, but they often reflect different stages of the land's history. Survey numbers come from the original land survey. Where land consolidation (ekatrikaran) was carried out, scattered holdings were regrouped and given new gat numbers. So a village may use gat numbers, survey numbers, or an older number that appears in old deeds but no longer matches the current map. When you compare an old document with today's map, check for a correspondence between old and new numbers rather than assuming the same number means the same land.

3. Reading the map

  • Each closed shape is a plot, and the number inside it is its gat or survey number.
  • A divided plot carries a hissa (sub-division) number, written after the main number, such as 45/2 or 45/1/A. An older sheet may not yet show a recent division.
  • Roads, cart tracks, streams and the gaothan (the inhabited part of the village) are drawn on the map as well.
  • Urban land is often recorded differently, under city survey (CTS) numbers and property cards rather than the 7/12.

4. Linking the map to the 7/12 and 8A

The 7/12 extract joins two village forms: Form VII records the holders, tenure and rights, and Form XII records the area, land use and crops. To use it, take the gat number and hissa from the map and open the 7/12 of that same village. The 8A extract works the other way around: it lists all the plots under one khata, so it helps when you start from a holder's account instead of a plot. Check the village name carefully, because the same gat number exists in every village.

5. Occupancy class and other entries

The 7/12 shows the occupancy class. Class 1 (bhogwatadar varg 1) is generally land the holder may transfer freely, while class 2 (varg 2) carries restrictions and may need permission before transfer. Government land and other categories appear in the record too, along with rights, charges and mutation references in the other-rights section. Read the current record and take advice from a revenue professional before relying on any entry, because these entries decide what can be done with the land.

6. Units of area

The 7/12 generally gives area in hectares and ares (H.R.), where 1 hectare is 100 ares; 1 acre is about 40.47 ares. People on the ground also speak in acres and gunthas, where 40 gunthas make 1 acre. Convert from the recorded value and state the conversion you used in any land schedule. Also note any pot kharab (uncultivable) area shown in the record, because it affects the usable area.

7. Why a viewer map is not yet a GIS map

A map on a screen is good for finding a plot, but it is not a file you can open with GPS points or a project layout. For that, the map must be georeferenced, meaning fitted to real coordinates such as WGS 84 latitude / longitude or UTM metres, and each plot digitized as a polygon carrying its number. The result should be checked on satellite imagery across the whole village: field edges, tracks and streams on the image should run along the plot lines.

8. Common uses

  • Seeing a gat on a satellite image in Google Earth
  • Checking which gat numbers fall inside a proposed solar, wind or industrial site
  • Listing the plots crossed by a road, pipeline or transmission line route
  • Preparing a gat-wise land schedule with village, area and class

9. Limits to keep in mind

The map shows the record as drawn. Recent divisions and new roads may not be on it yet, and the sheets of neighbouring villages do not always agree exactly along their shared edge. A digitized map is a planning base, not proof of title. The revenue record remains the legal source, and where a line decides ownership or compensation, a DGPS or Total Station survey on the ground is the next step.
