Land parcel maps for transmission lines: from route to khasra list

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 25-10-2026

A transmission line or an underground cable is a long, narrow project. A 30 km line can cross twenty villages in two or three tehsils, and every tower footing and every metre of the corridor sits on a plot that has an owner or a government land class. The route engineer thinks in coordinates and spans; the land team thinks in khasra numbers and khatas. A georeferenced land parcel map is the bridge between the two. This guide explains how it is used from the first route study to the land schedule.

1. Why a parcel map, not only satellite imagery

Satellite imagery shows fields, roads and buildings, but not who holds the land or how the revenue record classifies it. A khasra map placed on real coordinates shows the plot lines and numbers on top of the same imagery, so every point of the route can be tied to a plot in the land record. Without it, plot numbers are usually collected on site one by one, which is slow and easy to get wrong near village borders.

2. Villages crossed by the route

The first step is to overlay the proposed route on the village boundaries. This gives the list of villages, tehsils and districts the line passes through, the length of the route in each village and the revenue offices from which land-record extracts will be needed. Because the village boundary is built from the khasra plots themselves, the village list matches the plots that will later appear in the schedule.

3. Tower locations and the khasra under each tower

Angle points and tower locations come from the line survey or the route design. With the parcel map in the same coordinate system, each tower point falls inside one khasra - or close to a plot edge, which is worth knowing early. A simple table of tower number, coordinates, village and khasra number lets the land team start owner identification before the detailed survey is finished, and shows where a small shift of a tower would move it off a difficult plot.

4. Right-of-way corridor and affected area

The corridor width along the line depends on the voltage and on the rules the project follows, so it should come from the project's own design basis. Once that width is fixed, the corridor is drawn as a buffer around the route and cut with the khasra plots. The result is a khasra-wise list of the area inside the corridor and the length of the line over each plot - the starting point for compensation estimates and for the land schedule.

5. Government land, forest and other sensitive plots

Where the land record extract shows the land class, the plots on the route can be colour-coded: private land, government land that can be allotted, government land that cannot be allotted, and forest or other protected classes. This shows at a glance where separate permissions will be needed and where a route change might save time. As with any digitized map, the land class should always be confirmed against the current revenue record.

6. Files the different teams need

  • KML / KMZ - the route, towers and khasra plots in Google Earth for quick review and site visits.
  • DWG / DXF - plot lines and numbers on separate layers for the route and tower drawings in AutoCAD.
  • SHP - the same layers in GIS for the corridor buffer, area calculations and map sheets.
  • Excel - the land schedule: village, khasra number, land class, area of the plot and area or length inside the corridor.

7. Limits to keep in mind

  • Village maps were drawn at different times and scales; a georeferenced map is checked against satellite imagery, but it is still a planning base, not a legal demarcation.
  • Plots are often divided on the ground before the map is updated, so sub-divisions near tower points should be checked with the land record and on site.
  • For final tower pegging and plot-edge decisions, a DGPS or Total Station survey along the route is the right next step - we do those surveys as well.
