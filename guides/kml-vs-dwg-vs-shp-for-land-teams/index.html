KML vs DWG vs SHP vs Excel: which khasra map format does your land team need?

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 21-10-2026

When a land team orders a georeferenced khasra map, the first question is often: which file do we need? The honest answer is that different people on the same project need different formats. The map is the same - the same khasra polygons in the same place - but each format is built for a different tool and a different job. This guide explains the four formats we deliver and when to use each.

KML / KMZ - for the land team and the field

KML is the format of Google Earth. A KMZ is simply a zipped KML, often with labels or images inside. It is the easiest way for non-GIS people to see khasra boundaries on satellite imagery, on a laptop or on a phone.

  • Who uses it: land aggregators, project managers, site visit teams, clients who just want to look.
  • What it holds: khasra polygons, khasra numbers as labels, and a balloon with attributes such as area and land category.
  • Coordinates: always WGS 84 latitude / longitude.
  • Opens in: Google Earth Pro, Google Earth web, Google My Maps, and most mobile map apps.

Limitation: KML is for viewing and light editing. It is not the right file for engineering design or for spatial analysis across many villages.

DWG / DXF - for engineering and layout design

DWG is AutoCAD's own format and DXF is its open exchange version. Engineers designing solar module tables, wind turbine positions, roads, substations or transmission lines work in CAD, so they need the khasra boundaries as CAD geometry.

  • Who uses it: design engineers, CAD draughtsmen, EPC contractors.
  • What it holds: khasra boundaries as polylines on separate layers, khasra numbers as text, village boundary on its own layer.
  • Coordinates: projected UTM in metres (the correct zone for the area), so distances and areas measured in CAD are real ground values.
  • Opens in: AutoCAD, BricsCAD, ZWCAD, LibreCAD, and GIS software as a CAD layer.

Common mistake: a CAD file drawn in local or paper units cannot be overlaid on the project layout. Always check that the drawing is in real UTM coordinates - a khasra corner should read as a six-digit easting and a seven-digit northing.

Shapefile (SHP) - for GIS analysis

The Shapefile is the standard exchange format of GIS. One shapefile is really a set of files (.shp, .shx, .dbf, .prj and often .cpg) that must stay together in one folder.

  • Who uses it: GIS analysts, planners, consultants preparing land reports.
  • What it holds: one polygon per khasra with an attribute table - khasra number, village, area, land category and other fields.
  • Coordinates: defined in the .prj file, usually WGS 84 or UTM.
  • Opens in: QGIS (free), ArcGIS, Global Mapper and most GIS tools.

This is the format for questions like: which khasras fall inside the project boundary, how much government land lies within 2 km of the substation, or which plots a transmission corridor crosses. A clean shapefile has no overlaps and no gaps between neighbouring khasras, so areas add up correctly.

Excel - for the land budget and title work

The Excel sheet is the khasra list: one row per plot with its number, village, area and category. It is not a map, but it is what the land, legal and finance teams work from every day.

  • Who uses it: land managers, legal / title search teams, finance.
  • Typical use: tracking negotiation status, matching khasras with jamabandi / record-of-rights extracts, calculating the land budget.

Quick comparison

  • Need to see the land on satellite imagery: KML / KMZ.
  • Need to design a layout or a line on the land: DWG / DXF in UTM.
  • Need analysis, buffers, overlays or reports: SHP.
  • Need a list to track ownership and areas: Excel.

Why one order should include all four

On a real project the land team, the engineers and the GIS analyst work at the same time. If each converts the map on their own, small differences creep in - a shifted village, a lost attribute, a different coordinate system. Delivering KML, DWG, SHP and Excel from the same georeferenced geometry keeps every team on exactly the same khasra boundaries.

Remember that digitized land-record maps are a planning base, not a legal survey. Where a boundary matters legally, a DGPS or Total Station demarcation survey on the ground is the next step.
