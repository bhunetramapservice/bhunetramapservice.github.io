How to check government land in a village: records, maps and common mistakes

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 21-10-2026

Government land is often the key to a large solar park, a substation site or a road alignment. But the question "which plots in this village belong to the government?" has no single-click answer. The information sits in the land record, the plot shapes sit in the village map, and the two have to be brought together. This guide explains how that is done and where people usually go wrong.

1. Start with the record of rights

Every state keeps a written record for each plot - jamabandi, khatauni, 7/12 extract, RTC, patta, adangal or khatian, depending on the state (see our guide on land-record terms). For each khasra or survey number it lists the holder and, very often, the class of land. Government land usually appears under the name of the state government or a department, or under a category such as pasture (charagah / gochar), abadi, roads, ponds, forest or land reserved for public use.

  • Read the owner / holder column - the state government, a panchayat or a department.
  • Read the land class column - many states mark sarkari, banjar, charagah, nadi-nala, rasta or similar classes.
  • Note remarks such as allotted, leased, encroached or under acquisition - they change what the land can be used for.

2. Know the difference between kinds of government land

Not all government land can be used for a project. Land under ponds, rivers, drains, roads, cremation grounds, schools, pasture or forest is normally protected and cannot be allotted. Other government land (often recorded as uncultivable or waste land) may be allotted or leased under the state's own rules. The exact classes and rules are set by each state, so always read them in the current state rules or ask the local revenue office.

3. Put the categories on the village map

A list of khasra numbers alone does not show whether the government plots are next to each other, how big the contiguous block is or how it sits against the roads and the project boundary. For that, each khasra in the list is matched to its polygon on the georeferenced village map and coloured by category. Our standard colour code is:

  • Yellow - private / khatedari land
  • Red - government land that is not allotable (ponds, roads, pasture, public use and similar)
  • Green - government land that may be allotable

Other categories (leased, forest, disputed, already allotted) get their own colour on request. The result is a map where the land team sees at a glance where the usable government blocks are.

4. Check the map against the ground

  • Open the coloured KML on satellite imagery: a plot recorded as a pond should look like a pond, a road like a road.
  • Look for encroachment: houses or fields on plots recorded as government land are common and matter for allotment.
  • Check sub-divisions: a khasra split into 101/1 and 101/2 may have one private and one government part.
  • Compare areas: the polygon area should be close to the recorded area; a large difference needs a closer look.

5. Common mistakes

  • Using an old copy of the record - categories and allotments change, so use a current extract.
  • Matching khasra numbers to the wrong village - neighbouring villages often use the same numbers.
  • Treating every government plot as available - protected classes can never be allotted.
  • Treating a digitized map as a legal boundary - it is a planning base; demarcation on the ground needs a DGPS or Total Station survey.
