Jharkhand plot map: how to read khesra, khata and khatiyan on a village map

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 16-11-2026

In Jharkhand a piece of land is identified by the revenue village (mauza), the khata number and the plot number, which many people call the khesra number. The village map shows every plot with its number, while the khatiyan and the later Register II list who holds the land and how much of it. This guide explains how to read the map and the record together, and what is needed when the plots must be seen on a satellite image.

1. The terms you will meet

  • Mauza: the revenue village for which the map is drawn.
  • Plot / khesra number: the number of one parcel on the map.
  • Khata number: the holding; one khata can contain several plots.
  • Khatiyan: the survey record that lists the raiyat (holder), the father or husband name and the area of each plot.
  • Register II: the register that shows the current holders after mutations, so it can differ from the old khatiyan.
  • Raiyat: the person recorded as holding the land.

2. Reading the village map

The mauza map shows plot boundaries with the plot number inside each parcel, together with roads, streams, ponds and the village boundary. Plots that were later divided carry a sub-number or appear only in the record. A number that looks out of sequence on the map is worth checking against the khatiyan before you rely on it.

3. Linking the map to the record

Take the plot number from the map, choose the same district, block (anchal) and mauza in the land record, and search by plot or khata number. The khatiyan page then shows the area and holder recorded at survey, and Register II shows the present position. If the two disagree, the latest mutated entry in the revenue office is what counts.

4. Area units: acre, decimal, kattha

Jharkhand records mostly give area in acres and decimals. 100 decimals make 1 acre, and one decimal is about 40.47 square metres. Some records, mainly Register II entries, use local units such as kattha, chhatak and kari, and the size of a kattha is not the same everywhere. Keep the written value as it is, and convert it only with a stated conversion when you prepare a land schedule.

5. Government and common land

Land that belongs to the government, forest or the village community is recorded under its own entries, so a map alone does not tell you which plots are government land. Read the record of each plot and keep the category as written there. Where a decision depends on it, confirm the status with the revenue office.

6. From a viewer map to a GIS file

A map that you can only look at on a screen is useful for finding a plot, but it cannot be opened with GPS points or used in a project layout. For that the map has to be georeferenced, meaning fitted to real coordinates such as WGS 84 latitude and longitude or UTM metres, and every plot has to be a closed polygon that carries its number. The result should then be compared with satellite imagery: field bunds, roads and streams should sit on the plot edges.

7. Common uses

  • Seeing a plot on a satellite image in Google Earth
  • Listing the plots inside a proposed solar, mining-support or industrial site
  • Listing the plots crossed by a road, canal, pipeline or transmission line
  • Preparing a plot-wise land schedule with mauza, khata, area and holder

8. Limits to keep in mind

The map shows the record as drawn at survey. Later divisions and new roads may not appear on it, and neighbouring mauzas do not always agree exactly along a shared edge. A digitized map is a planning base, not proof of title. Where a boundary line decides ownership or compensation, a DGPS or Total Station survey on the ground is the right step.
