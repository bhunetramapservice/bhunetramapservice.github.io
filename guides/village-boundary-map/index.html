Village boundary map: where it comes from and how to use it on a land project

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 22-10-2026

Almost every land project starts with the same question: which villages does the site touch? A solar park, a wind farm, a transmission line or a road alignment crosses village limits, and every village means a different land record, a different patwari or talathi and often a different tehsil. A reliable village boundary map answers that question in minutes. This guide explains where village boundaries come from, why two boundary maps of the same village rarely agree, and how to use one on a project.

1. Two kinds of village boundary

In India you will usually meet two different outlines for the same village:

  • Census or administrative boundaries - drawn for counting and planning, often at a small scale. They are good for an overview of a district or for finding which village a point falls in, but their lines can be tens or hundreds of metres away from the real plot edges.
  • Cadastral boundaries - the outer edge of the village map itself, made of the plots (khasra, survey or dag numbers) recorded in the land records. This is the boundary the revenue department works with, because it follows the plots that are actually owned, leased or allotted.

For a quick look either one will do. For anything that involves land - acquisition, lease, allotment of government land, right-of-way - the cadastral boundary is the one that matters.

2. How a cadastral village boundary is built

We build the village boundary directly from the khasra plots of the digitized and georeferenced village map. All plots of the village are joined into one shape, and the outer edge of that shape is the boundary. Internal gaps between plots are not treated as holes, and nothing is added outside the plots - no buffer, no smoothing. The result is a line that sits exactly on the outer edges of the outer plots, so every khasra of the village lies inside it and the boundary and the plot lines match in CAD and GIS.

When the neighbouring villages are mapped in the same job, small gaps along roads and drains between two villages can be closed and detached plots joined, but a boundary is never drawn over a neighbouring village's land. Where a whole tehsil or project area is delivered, the village outlines fit together side by side like the plots inside them.

3. Why maps of the same village differ

  • Scale and age - many village maps were drawn decades ago at 1:4,000 or similar scales, and census outlines were often traced from smaller-scale sheets.
  • Georeferencing - a scanned map has to be fitted to real coordinates; a poor fit can shift a whole village.
  • Changes on the ground - rivers move, new roads and canals cut plots, and villages are split or merged.
  • Unsurveyed land - forest, river beds or large government blocks sometimes sit outside every village map.

That is why we check every georeferenced village against satellite imagery: field edges, roads and ponds on the image should line up with the plot lines on the map.

4. How the boundary is delivered

The village boundary is not a separate file that can get lost. It travels inside the same files as the khasra plots:

  • KML - the first placemark of the village folder, so it shows in Google Earth together with every khasra.
  • DWG / DXF - its own layer (VILLAGE_BOUNDARY), which can be switched on or off in AutoCAD.
  • SHP - the first record of the khasra shapefile, marked VILLAGE BOUNDARY, with a style file for QGIS.

5. Using it on a project

  • Overlay the project boundary or the line route on the village boundaries to list every village it touches.
  • Split the project area village by village, so land-record extracts can be requested from the right offices.
  • Check the area of each village part against the recorded areas before you count acres or hectares.
  • Hand the same files to the land team, the engineers and the GIS analyst, so everyone works on the same lines.

Remember that a digitized village boundary is a planning base, not a legal demarcation. Where a boundary line decides ownership or compensation, a DGPS or Total Station survey on the ground is the next step - we do those surveys too.
