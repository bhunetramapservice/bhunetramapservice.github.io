Rajasthan Bhu-Naksha on a satellite map: how to read a khasra map online

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 26-10-2026

Many people in Rajasthan search for the Bhu-Naksha "on a satellite map". The reason is simple: the village map on the land-record portal shows plot lines and khasra numbers on a plain sheet, while the land itself is easiest to recognise on satellite imagery - the field bunds, the road, the well, the pond. Putting the two together tells you which khasra is which field. This guide explains how to read the Rajasthan khasra map online, what the jamabandi adds, and what it takes to see the khasra lines on satellite imagery.

1. Finding the village on Bhu-Naksha Rajasthan

The public Bhu-Naksha viewer is organised by district, tehsil and village. Choose them in that order and the village map opens; larger villages are sometimes split into several map sheets. Clicking a plot shows its khasra number and, where linked, a short extract of the record. The names follow the land record, so a village may be spelt differently from a road sign or the census - if a name does not appear, look for a spelling variant or check the tehsil once more.

2. Reading the khasra map

  • Khasra number - every plot carries a number; sub-divided plots get numbers such as 125/1, 125/2 or a letter after the parent number.
  • Plot lines - the closed shapes are the plots as recorded on the village map; long thin plots are often roads (rasta), canals or drains, which are numbered too.
  • Village edge - the outer line of the outer plots is the cadastral village boundary, which can differ from census or road-map outlines.
  • Scale - the map was drawn on paper at a fixed scale, so small plots near the village habitation (abadi) can be hard to read on screen.

3. What the jamabandi adds

The map shows where a khasra lies; it does not show who holds it. That comes from the jamabandi (record of rights), available through Apna Khata. It lists the khata, the holders and their shares, the area and the land class for each khasra. Areas in the record are usually in hectares, while people on the ground often speak in bigha - and the size of a bigha differs between regions of Rajasthan, so always convert from the recorded unit, not from a rule of thumb.

4. Why the plain map does not sit on satellite imagery

A village map is a drawing made in its own paper coordinates. To place it on satellite imagery it has to be georeferenced: fitted to real-world coordinates (WGS 84 latitude / longitude or UTM metres) with control points such as plot corners, road junctions and village edges that can be recognised on both. A rough fit can leave a whole village tens of metres off, which is enough to put a field into the neighbouring khasra. That is why the fit should be checked on the imagery itself - field edges and roads on the image should run along the khasra lines across the whole village, not only in one corner.

5. Seeing the khasra lines on satellite imagery

Once the map is georeferenced and every plot is digitized as a polygon with its number, it can be opened in Google Earth as a KML file. The khasra lines and numbers then lie over the satellite image, and you can click a plot, measure it, or draw a project boundary on top. The same plots can be delivered as DWG / DXF for AutoCAD, SHP for QGIS or ArcGIS, and an Excel list of khasra numbers and areas - so the land team, the engineers and the GIS analyst all work on the same lines.

6. Limits to keep in mind

  • Village maps were drawn decades ago; roads, canals and sub-divisions made later may not appear yet.
  • Satellite imagery has its own small offsets and dates - a recent field change may not show.
  • A digitized, georeferenced map is a planning base, not a legal demarcation. Where a line decides ownership or compensation, a DGPS or Total Station survey on the ground is the next step - we do those surveys too.
