Uttar Pradesh village map: how to read the khasra (gata) map online

BHU-NETRA Map Service · 31-10-2026

In Uttar Pradesh the village map is known by several names: shajra, naksha, bhu-naksha, or simply the village map. On it, every plot of land is drawn and numbered, and that number is called the khasra number or gata number. The two words are used for the same plot in everyday UP land work. This guide explains how to read the map, how it connects to the khatauni, and what to do when you need it on a satellite image or in a GIS file.

1. Where the map comes from

The maps were drawn during land surveys and settlement work and are kept up by the revenue department. In the village, the lekhpal works with the map and the records; above that sit the tehsil and the district. Many village maps can be viewed online on the UP Bhunaksha portal by choosing district, tehsil and village. Land records can be seen on the state's Bhulekh portal. These are official state services; the copy you see there is the place to start.

2. Reading the gata numbers

  • Each closed shape on the map is one plot, and the number inside it is its gata (khasra) number.
  • Numbers usually run in a sequence across the village, so neighbouring plots often have close numbers.
  • A divided plot may carry a number with a letter or a slash, such as 112A or 112/1. An older sheet may still show the undivided plot even though the record has changed.
  • Very small plots may have the number written beside them with a short line, because it does not fit inside.

3. What else the map shows

Not every numbered plot is a field. Paths (chak road or rasta), drains, canals, ponds and the abadi (the inhabited part of the village) are also drawn on the map. A chak road crossing a land block is a separate plot and does not belong to the holders on either side, so it matters when you plan a project or check a boundary.

4. Linking the map to the khatauni

The map shows where a plot is, not who holds it. For that you need the khatauni, the record of rights, which lists gata numbers under each khata with the holders, shares and area. Take the gata number from the map and look it up in the khatauni. Check the village name carefully as well: many UP villages have similar names, and the same gata number exists in every village.

5. Land classes in the record

The khatauni also places land in a class (shreni). Class 1-ka is the land of a bhumidhar with transferable rights, the usual private holding. Other classes cover holders with more limited rights, and land vested in the gram sabha, including ponds, paths, pasture and other land not available for ordinary transfer. Before planning on a plot, read its class in the current record and take advice from a revenue professional, because the exact class and any restriction decide what can be done with it.

6. Units of area

Current records generally give area in hectares, while people on the ground still talk in bigha, and the size of a bigha differs from one part of the state to another. Always convert from the recorded area, not from a spoken bigha value, and state which conversion you used in any land schedule.

7. Why a viewer map is not yet a GIS map

A map on a portal screen is good for finding a plot, but it does not give you a file you can open with GPS points or a project layout. For that the map has to be georeferenced, meaning fitted to real coordinates such as WGS 84 latitude / longitude or UTM metres, and each plot digitized as a polygon carrying its number. The result should be checked on satellite imagery across the whole village: field edges, chak roads and canals on the image should run along the plot lines.

8. Common uses

  • Seeing your plot on a satellite image in Google Earth
  • Checking which gata numbers fall inside a proposed solar, industrial or warehouse site
  • Listing the plots crossed by a road, pipeline or transmission line route
  • Preparing a gata-wise land schedule with village, area and land class

9. Limits to keep in mind

The map shows the record as drawn. Recent divisions, consolidation changes and new roads may not be on it yet, and old sheets of neighbouring villages do not always agree exactly along their shared edge. A digitized map is a planning base, not proof of title. The revenue record remains the legal source, and where a line decides ownership or compensation, a DGPS or Total Station survey on the ground is the next step.
